The Gaps I Look For

Five reads your dashboard is averaging out of existence.

Each one I've found in real data — and each was invisible in the aggregate metrics the team was already watching.

01

Your middle cohort is churning — and your dashboard is hiding it

Your top 20% mask the bleed. The middle cohort — highest growth potential, highest sensitivity to friction — drops off in silence while aggregate metrics stay green. That's the segment that moves the number.

Starbucks · 3.2M loyalty records — 14% retention lift invisible in aggregate
02

Price sensitivity is non-linear — you're missing the cliff

Average elasticity gives you a false ceiling. Demand breaks at a threshold, not a slope. By the time blended ROAS shows it, you've spent past the efficient return window. Segment-level curves catch it weeks earlier.

British Airways · 12 fare classes — regression-discontinuity elasticity model
03

Your attribution is backward-looking — every spend decision runs on stale signal

Most models tell you what correlated with a conversion. Without holdout groups and causal validation, you're measuring organic trend and calling it impact. MMM without a causal scaffold is the same error at a bigger budget.

British Airways · retargeting saturation — ROAS turned negative before detection
04

Choice architecture is costing conversions you can't trace

Cognitive overload at decision points isn't a UX issue — it's behavioral economics. Too many options or high-friction flows trigger System 1 shutdown: people abandon because the decision is hard, not because they don't want the product.

Starbucks · DiD causal experiment — Prof. Nina Mazar complexity framework
05

NPS is a slide number — behavioral funnels are the actual signal

Stated preference and revealed behavior diverge under real conditions. A customer who scores you a 9 and churns two weeks later is common. Survey metrics without behavioral anchoring give you confidence in data that doesn't predict anything.

Newdia Co. · built the first behavioral NPS + CAC funnel the business ever had

So what

The decision it changed: a targeted menu-simplification bet for one segment — not a blanket change — backed by causal evidence instead of a hunch.
